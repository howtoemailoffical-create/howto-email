Email is a way to send written messages over the internet. People use it at school, at work, and in everyday life. An email can contain text, pictures, documents, and links.

Learning to use email well means more than knowing which button to press. It also means writing clearly, treating people respectfully, and keeping personal information safe. These habits are sometimes called **email etiquette** or **netiquette**. Netiquette means being polite and responsible when communicating online.

This guide explains the basics in simple steps.

## 1. Understand the parts of an email

Most email messages have these parts:

- **From:** The email address that appears to have sent the message.
- **To:** The person or people receiving the message.
- **Subject:** A short description of what the email is about.
- **Message body:** The main text you write.
- **Attachment:** A file included with the message, such as a document or picture.
- **CC:** Sends a visible copy to someone who should be informed.
- **BCC:** Sends a copy without showing that person's address to other recipients.

An **email address** usually has a name, the @ symbol, and a domain, such as `student@example.com`. The domain is the part after the @ symbol.

Check addresses carefully. A small typing mistake can send your message to the wrong person.

## 2. Set up and protect your email account

Your email account is personal. Use a strong, unique password and do not share it with friends or classmates. If your email service offers **two-factor authentication**, turn it on. This adds another step when someone tries to sign in.

Keep recovery information up to date so you can regain access if you forget your password. Sign out of your account on shared or public computers. Do not let someone else use your account to send messages.

If you are a student, follow your school's rules for school-issued email accounts. A school account may be intended for schoolwork and school communication.

## 3. Decide whether email is the right way to communicate

Email is useful when you need to ask a question, share information, send a document, or keep a written record of a conversation.

Email is not always immediate. The person receiving it may be in class, working, or away from their computer. Do not assume someone has read your message just because you sent it.

For an emergency, follow your school or family's emergency procedures instead of relying on email.

## 4. Write a clear subject line

The subject line tells the reader why you are writing. Keep it short and specific.

Good subject lines include:

- Question About Homework Due Friday
- Request to Meet About Class Project
- Permission Form for School Trip
- Thank You for Your Help

Avoid leaving the subject blank. Avoid subjects that are too vague, such as “Important” or “Question,” when you can be more specific.

## 5. Start with a polite greeting

A greeting helps your message feel respectful. You can begin with:

- Hello Ms. Rivera,
- Good morning Mr. Lee,
- Hello,
- Dear School Office,

Use the name or title the person prefers. If you are not sure how to address a teacher or another adult, use a respectful greeting.

After the greeting, move to the reason for your email.

## 6. Explain why you are writing

State your reason early. Use short sentences and separate different ideas into paragraphs.

A clear email usually includes:

1. A greeting.
2. A sentence explaining why you are writing.
3. Any important details or questions.
4. A polite closing.
5. Your name.

You do not need to use complicated words. Simple, correct language is easier to understand.

### Example: Asking a teacher a question

**Subject: Question About Friday's Assignment**

Hello Ms. Rivera,

I am writing to ask about the assignment due Friday. Could you please clarify whether we should submit it online or bring a printed copy to class?

Thank you for your help.

Sincerely,  
Jordan

This example is short, respectful, and clear.

## 7. Use a respectful tone

Your **tone** is the way your words may sound to another person. In email, the reader cannot hear your voice or see your facial expressions. Words can sometimes seem more serious or less friendly than you intended.

To communicate respectfully:

- Say “please” when making a request.
- Say “thank you” when someone helps you.
- Ask questions politely.
- Avoid insults, name-calling, and personal attacks.
- Do not use all capital letters to express anger.
- Avoid sending a message while you are upset.

You can disagree with someone without being rude. Focus on the issue instead of criticizing the person.

Before sending a difficult message, read it again and ask yourself whether it is calm, fair, and clear.

## 8. Use spelling, punctuation, and paragraphs

You do not need to write perfectly, but you should make your email easy to read.

Start sentences with capital letters. Use periods and question marks where they belong. Break up long blocks of text into shorter paragraphs.

Use the spelling and grammar tools available in your email service, but read the message yourself too. Automatic suggestions are not always correct.

For school or professional emails, avoid unnecessary abbreviations, excessive emojis, and texting shortcuts. Use complete words whenever possible.

## 9. Know when to use Reply, Reply All, and Forward

These buttons do different things.

**Reply** sends your answer to the sender, and sometimes to other recipients depending on your email application.

**Reply All** sends your answer to the sender and the other people included in the conversation. Use it only when everyone needs your response. Check the recipient list before sending.

**Forward** sends a copy of a message to someone else. Before forwarding, make sure you have permission to share its contents. Emails may include private information.

When you respond to an email, answer the questions you were asked. If there are several questions, it may help to answer them in the same order.

## 10. Understand CC and BCC

**CC** stands for carbon copy. People in the CC field receive the email, and their addresses are visible to other recipients.

**BCC** stands for blind carbon copy. BCC recipients receive the message, but their addresses are hidden from the other recipients.

Use these fields carefully. Do not include extra people just because you can. When sending a message to a large group of people who do not know one another, BCC can help protect their email addresses.

BCC does not make a message completely private. A recipient can still copy, screenshot, or forward its contents.

## 11. Be careful with attachments and links

An attachment is a file sent with an email. Before attaching a file, make sure it is the correct one and that it is appropriate to share.

Use clear file names so the recipient can recognize the document. If you mention an attachment in your message, confirm that it is attached before sending.

Be cautious when opening attachments or clicking links in emails you receive. Even a message that appears to come from someone you know could be fake or sent from a compromised account.

If a message asks you to sign in, provide a password, or send personal information, verify the request through a trusted method. Do not rely only on the sender name or the link inside the email.

## 12. Protect private information

Think before you share information by email. Avoid sending passwords, one-time login codes, financial details, or sensitive personal information through ordinary email.

Do not share another person's address, phone number, grades, or personal information without permission.

Email is not the same as a private conversation. Messages can be forwarded, saved, copied, or viewed by people who have access to an account. School and workplace email systems may also be subject to organizational rules and monitoring.

Only send information you are comfortable sharing with the intended recipient, and use an approved secure method when sensitive information must be exchanged.

## 13. Learn to recognize suspicious emails

Some emails try to trick people into giving away information or opening unsafe links. This is called **phishing**.

Warning signs can include:

- A request for your password or login code.
- A message that pressures you to act immediately.
- An unexpected attachment or link.
- An address that does not match the organization it claims to represent.
- A request to keep the message secret or send money.

These signs do not prove an email is malicious, and some scams look very convincing.

If you are unsure, do not click the link or open the attachment. Ask a trusted adult, teacher, or school technology staff member for help. If a message claims to be from a company or school, contact them using a website or phone number you already know is legitimate.

## 14. Respect other people's time

Not everyone checks email at the same time. Allow a reasonable amount of time for a reply, especially outside school or working hours.

If you need an answer by a certain date, explain that politely and give the recipient enough notice when possible.

Avoid sending the same message repeatedly. If you have not received a response after a reasonable period, a short, respectful follow-up is appropriate.

### Example: Following up

**Subject: Follow-Up on Class Project Question**

Hello Mr. Lee,

I wanted to follow up on my question about the class project. When you have time, could you please let me know which topic I should use?

Thank you.

Jordan

## 15. Avoid spam and unnecessary messages

**Spam** is unwanted or unsolicited messaging, often sent in large amounts.

Do not send chain messages, repeated advertisements, or messages that have no useful purpose. Do not sign other people up for mailing lists without permission.

If you receive unwanted messages, use your email provider's spam or block features when appropriate. Do not respond to suspicious spam messages.

## 16. Keep your inbox organized

An **inbox** is where new messages usually appear. Keeping it organized makes important information easier to find.

Useful habits include:

- Check your inbox regularly.
- Read messages carefully before responding.
- Mark important messages so you can find them later.
- Use folders or labels if your email service supports them.
- Archive messages you may need later.
- Delete messages you no longer need, following school or organization rules.

**Archive** usually removes a message from the inbox without deleting it. **Delete** moves a message toward removal, depending on your email service.

Be careful before deleting school instructions, receipts, or other information you may need again.

## 17. Check your email before sending

Take a few seconds to review your message.

**Before you press Send, ask yourself:**

- Am I sending this to the correct person?
- Is the subject line clear?
- Did I explain why I am writing?
- Is my message polite and easy to understand?
- Did I answer the questions I needed to answer?
- Did I attach the correct file, if needed?
- Am I sharing any information that should stay private?
- Would I be comfortable if the recipient saved or forwarded this message?

If something does not look right, fix it before sending.

## 18. What to do if you make a mistake

Everyone makes mistakes when writing email. If you send a message to the wrong person, forget an attachment, or write something unclear, respond calmly.

If you forgot an attachment, send a short follow-up with the correct file. If your message was confusing, clarify what you meant. If you were rude, apologize and communicate more respectfully.

If you accidentally shared private information, tell a trusted adult, teacher, or the appropriate school or organization contact promptly. They can help you decide what to do next.

Do not assume that deleting a sent message removes it from the recipient's inbox. Some services offer an undo-send feature, but it usually works only for a short time and is not guaranteed to recall a delivered email.

## 19. Remember that online communication affects real people

Behind every email address is a person. Treat that person with the same respect you would show in a classroom, at home, or at work.

Do not use email to bully, threaten, embarrass, or spread rumors about others. Do not share someone else's messages or pictures without permission.

If you receive a hurtful or threatening message, do not respond with another hurtful message. Save the message if needed and tell a trusted adult or appropriate school staff member.

Being responsible online includes respecting other people's privacy, boundaries, and feelings.

## 20. Build good email habits

Good email habits are simple:

1. Write a useful subject line.
2. Greet the recipient politely.
3. Explain your reason for writing.
4. Keep your message clear and respectful.
5. Check the recipients, links, and attachments.
6. Protect private information.
7. Read your message before sending.
8. Give people reasonable time to reply.

You do not have to memorize every rule immediately. With practice, writing a clear email becomes easier.

## Final reminder

**Think before you send.** Email is a useful way to learn, ask questions, and stay connected. When you write clearly, treat others respectfully, and protect personal information, you help make online communication better for everyone.
